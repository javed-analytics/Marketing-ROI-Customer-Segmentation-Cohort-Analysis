## Executive Summary

Marketing investments were analyzed to determine how effectively they drive revenue across channels and customer segments. The analysis revealed that increasing marketing spend yields diminishing ROI, and some channels significantly outperform others in efficiency. A small group of high-value customers contributes a disproportionate share of revenue, highlighting an opportunity for targeted retention and loyalty strategies. Meanwhile, cohort analysis shows that newer customer cohorts are highly active but have lower spend so far, whereas older cohorts generated higher lifetime value but are less engaged now. These insights point to clear actions: rebalance marketing budgets toward high-ROI channels, nurture valuable customer segments, and tailor strategies to sustain customer engagement over time.

## Project Objectives

• ROI Analysis: Quantify overall marketing Return on Investment (ROI) and examine how ROI changes under different budget scenarios (low, base, high spend).

• Channel Performance: Attribute sales revenue to marketing channels and evaluate each channel’s ROI to identify top-performing vs. underperforming channels.

• Customer Segmentation: Segment customers into High, Mid, and Low value tiers based on total spend percentiles, to understand value distribution and segment characteristics.

• Cohort Analysis: Analyze monthly customer cohorts by join date, assessing their cumulative value and engagement (active vs. at-risk identifying potential churn) to uncover retention trends and lifecycle patterns.

## Data Overview

The dataset covers 2,214 customers of a retail campaign (dating from August 2012 to June 2014). For each customer, we have demographic info and detailed purchase history (total spend across product categories). Marketing data includes the total revenue generated ($1.34M in customer purchases) and marketing spend allocated across four channels: Web, Catalog, Deals, and Store. Each purchase was attributed to channels proportionally - for example, if a customer made 50% of their purchases online, 50% of that customer’s spend was attributed to the Web channel. This produced an attributed revenue for each channel, enabling calculation of ROI by channel. The data also contains several pre-calculated fields such as customer’s spend per product category (e.g., MntWines, MntFruits), number of purchases by channel, recency (days since last purchase), and campaign response metrics. Additional features were manually engineered to support ROI calculation, customer segmentation and cohort analysis.

## Methodology \& Metrics Used

**We calculated ROI** = (Revenue - Spend) / Spend for the marketing budget overall and per channel. A baseline marketing budget ($103K) was set to 7.7% of total revenue ($1.34M) based on industry benchmarks (marketing spend is ~7–10% of revenue on average). To test sensitivity, we modeled a Low budget (20% below baseline) and High budget (20% above baseline) while keeping revenue constant, to observe ROI changes with spend.

**Channel-wise ROI** was computed using attributed revenue: for each channel, revenue attributed to that channel was compared to that channel’s share of the marketing spend (the base budget was split as 45% Web, 25% Store, 20% Catalog, 10% Deals in the data).

**For customer segmentation**, we used percentile thresholds on total spend (MntTotal): customers above the 80th percentile of spend ($1,175+) are “High” value, those between the 50th and 80th percentile ($400–$1,175) are “Mid”, and the rest (bottom 50%) are “Low” value. This segmentation tiers the customers into roughly 20% High, 30% Mid, 50% Low, and we aggregated the number of customers and revenue contribution of each tier.

**For cohort analysis**, we grouped customers by their join month (month of first purchase) to track how each cohort contributes to revenue and how engaged they remain. Key metrics computed per cohort include: - New Customers: number of customers who joined that month. - Cohort Value: total revenue generated by that cohort to date. - Average Value per Customer: cohort’s total revenue divided by number of customers (to gauge per-customer lifetime value in that cohort). - Value Index: the average value of that cohort’s customers relative to the overall customer average (an index >1.0 means the cohort’s customers spend more than average).

**Engagement \& Behavioral Metrics:** Engagement was measured using multiple recency, activity, channel, and campaign-related indicators. Active Rate = % of cohort customers who made a purchase in the last 30 days (indicating recent activity), and At-Risk Rate = % who have not purchased in 90 days (indicating potential churn). These recency-based rates help assess how well cohorts are retained over time. Additional engagement signals include channel engagement mix (average share of purchases via web, catalog, store, and deals), total monthly web visits, campaign response rate, campaign acceptance rate, and complaint rate, providing a holistic view of how actively and effectively each cohort interacts with the brand across touchpoints.

These engagement-based metrics help assess customer retention, responsiveness to marketing efforts, and channel preferences over time.

All analysis was conducted in Excel, using formulas and pivot tables to compute the above metrics and summarize insights.

## Key Findings

• **ROI \& Budget Sensitivity:** Marketing ROI is inversely related to budget size. At the low-budget scenario (20% under baseline), ROI was highest (~15.2 or 1523% return), while the base case ROI was ~12.0. At the high-budget scenario (+20% spend), ROI dropped to ~9.8. In other words, spending significantly above the ~7.7% of revenue benchmark yielded diminishing returns, the extra spend did not generate proportional revenue. This trend implies the company should be cautious with increasing marketing budgets: beyond a certain point, additional marketing dollars yield much lower incremental profit. The optimal spend level appears to be near the baseline; cutting spend slightly could improve efficiency (higher ROI), but cutting too much might risk losing revenue volume.

• **Channel Attribution \& Performance:** There are major disparities in ROI by channel. The Store channel delivered the highest performance. It accounted for about 39% of attributed sales revenue while using only 25% of the marketing budget, resulting in an ROI of nearly 19× (highest among channels). Catalog and Deals channels were also efficient: each generated roughly their proportional share of revenue (Catalog 23% of revenue on 20% of spend; Deals 11% of revenue on 10% of spend), with ROI around 13–14×. Web, however, was an outlier in the opposite direction: the Web channel consumed the largest portion of spend (45% of budget) but only produced about 27% of revenue, giving it the lowest ROI at around 6.7×. In absolute terms, the Web channel had the highest spend ($46K) but the smallest profit margin, indicating over-investment in Web campaigns relative to returns.

• **Customer Segmentation (High/Mid/Low Value):** Customer value is heavily concentrated in the top segment. The top 20% of customers (High-Value segment) contributed about 52% of total revenue ($703K of $1.34M). In contrast, the bottom 50% of customers (Low-Value segment) together contributed only ~9% of revenue ($123K). The Mid-Value segment (30% of customers) accounted for the remaining ~39% of sales. This uneven distribution underscores a classic 80/20 rule: a small fraction of customers drives the majority of revenue. The average High-Value customer spent $1,586, which is about 2× the overall average and 14× more than an average Low-Value customer ($111). High-Value customers likely have higher frequency or larger basket sizes, making them critical to the business. By definition, High-Value customers had lifetime spend above $1,175 (the 80th percentile threshold), whereas Low-Value customers spent less than $400 total. This segmentation reveals an opportunity to focus marketing and retention efforts on the high-value tier (to maintain their loyalty and spending) and devise strategies to uplift mid-tier customers into the high tier. It also suggests the company should not overspend on acquiring many low-value customers without strategies to increase their value, as they generate relatively little revenue.

• **Cohort Analysis (Monthly Join Cohorts):** Customer cohorts exhibit clear patterns in both value and engagement. Cohorts acquired in earlier periods (e.g. late 2012 and 2013) have had more time to spend and thus show higher total value per customer (value index >1, meaning above-average lifetime spend). For example, customers who joined in 2012 have spent about 1.2–1.3× the average customers spend. However, these older cohorts also have lower recent engagement – on average only ~25–30% of 2012–2013 cohort members are still “Active” (recent purchasers), and a higher proportion (15–20%) are at-risk of churning (no purchase in 90+ days). In contrast, newer cohorts (customers who joined in early 2014) have lower cumulative spend (value index ~0.8–0.9, below average so far), simply because they’ve been with the company for a shorter time. But these new cohorts show higher activity rates – often 35–40% of those customers made a purchase in the last month, and very few (around 5–10%) are at-risk yet. Essentially, as cohorts age, their average lifetime value grows but their engagement wanes: many early cohort customers have either slowed down or stopped purchasing over time, whereas recent cohorts are in an earlier, more active stage of the customer lifecycle. Additionally, the cohort analysis showed that the company’s customer acquisition peaked in 2013 (over half of all customers joined during 2013), and slowed slightly in the first half of 2014. This large 2013 cohort is contributing substantially to current revenue but will require ongoing engagement to prevent attrition. The channel preferences of cohorts were relatively consistent, with a slight trend that newer customers had an even higher share of their purchases in stores (e.g. 42% store for 2014 cohort’s vs ~38% for 2012 cohorts) and marginally less via catalog, indicating a gradual shift toward in-store and possibly online channels over time. Overall, the cohort analysis highlights the importance of retention efforts: to continue deriving revenue from older cohorts (reactivating at-risk customers) and to nurture newer cohorts so that their lifetime value grows to its full potential.

• **Digital Engagement \& Campaign Responsiveness:** Across cohorts, average monthly web visits remain relatively high (approximately 350–770 visits per cohort), indicating sustained digital engagement even among older customer groups. However, campaign response rates decline over time, from ~25–37% in earlier cohorts to below 10% in more recent or lower-engagement cohorts, suggesting diminishing responsiveness despite continued browsing behavior. Campaign acceptance rates remain moderate (generally 20–40%), implying that while fewer customers actively respond, those who do engage are still willing to convert when offers are relevant. Complaint rates remain consistently low (0–5%) across all cohorts, indicating that reduced campaign response is not driven by dissatisfaction, but more likely by offer fatigue or misalignment of messaging with customer needs.

## Business Recommendations

• **Reallocate Budget to High-ROI Channels:** Adjust the marketing mix to reduce spend on low-ROI channels (notably the Web channel) and increase investment in high-ROI areas like Store and Catalog. By shifting budget toward the channels that yield better returns, the company can improve overall marketing efficiency (for instance, even a 10% budget reallocation from Web to Store could materially boost profit given Store’s ROI is nearly 3× Web’s).

• **Optimize Web Marketing Strategy:** Investigate why the Web channel underperformed – e.g. high customer acquisition costs, low conversion rates, or attribution issues. Improve the ROI of Web campaigns by refining targeting, creatives, or conversion funnels. If improvements are limited, continue limiting spend in Web to only the most effective campaigns and redirect funds elsewhere.

• **Focus on High-Value Customers:** The top-tier customers are driving over half of revenue, so retaining and growing this segment is critical. Implement loyalty programs, exclusive offers, or VIP experiences to reward High-Value customers and increase their lifetime value even further. Ensure customer service and satisfaction remain high for this group to prevent defection, as losing a top customer would significantly impact revenue.

• **Grow the Mid-Value Segment:** The Mid segment represents a substantial 30% of customers and ~38% of revenue – they are the next best customers after the top tier. Deploy targeted marketing (personalized product recommendations, upsell campaigns, etc.) to encourage higher spend from Mid-Value customers. The goal is to graduate more mid-tier customers into the High-Value tier over time by increasing their purchase frequency or average order value. This could involve tailored promotions or engagement based on their preferences to boost their loyalty and spend.

• **Retention and Reactivation Campaigns:** Given that older cohorts have many lapsed or at-risk customers, initiate reactivation campaigns (special discounts, win-back emails, or calls) aimed at customers who haven’t purchased in over 3 or 6 months. Even re-engaging a fraction of the at-risk group can recover revenue at relatively low cost (since they are already in the database). Simultaneously, maintain strong onboarding and retention programs for new cohorts – for example, follow-up with new customers soon after their first purchase with incentives for a second purchase, to solidify habits and increase their lifetime value early.

• **Right-Size Marketing Budget:** Avoid assuming that more spending will linearly produce more revenue. The sensitivity analysis indicates an optimal range around the current budget. Marketing leadership should set budgets with ROI in mind, perhaps capping or carefully scrutinizing spend beyond the current level. Any significant increase in budget should be tied to specific campaigns or channels with proven potential, or tested incrementally to ensure they truly drive additional profitable revenue. By controlling budget creep, the company can maintain a high overall ROI and invest excess funds into other high-return opportunities (or the bottom line).

• **Continuous Monitoring \& Testing:** Integrate these analyses into an ongoing dashboard. Track channel ROI quarterly to spot shifts (e.g. if a channel’s performance improves or deteriorates). Monitor customer segment metrics – if the high-value segment starts shrinking or low-value grows, investigate causes (like losing big spenders or bringing in too many low-value leads). Also, extend cohort tracking forward: observe how cohorts from 2014 and onward perform as they mature, to validate lifetime value assumptions. Continuously test different budget allocations and marketing tactics on a small scale (A/B tests or regional pilots) to see their impact on ROI before scaling company-wide. This iterative approach will ensure marketing spend is always aligned with the most profitable outcomes.

## Limitations

• **Attribution Assumptions:** The revenue attribution model is based on each channel’s share of purchases, which is a proxy. It assumes each channel contributed proportionally to revenue, but in reality, some channels might influence sales beyond what a simple split suggests (e.g. multi-touch attribution is not captured). The ROI per channel here should be interpreted with that simplification in mind.

• **Static Revenue in Sensitivity Analysis:** The budget sensitivity looked at ROI assuming total revenue stays at $1.35M. In practice, cutting the marketing budget might also reduce revenue (and increasing budget might increase revenue) i.e. there are diminishing returns, but not strictly fixed revenue. The analysis doesn’t model the complex response of sales to spend; it provides a directional insight (that ROI likely declines with overspending). Actual budgeting decisions should consider revenue response curves and marginal ROI, which require further data or testing.

• **No Transaction Date:** The dataset contains customer join date (Dt\_Customer) and total spend (MntTotal), but it does not include individual transaction dates. Because transaction dates were not available, cohort value is measured as lifetime spend to date rather than time-based revenue; engagement was therefore analyzed using recency-based active and at-risk rates.

• **Time Frame and Cohort Duration:** The data is a snapshot up to 2014. Cohort analysis is limited to a relatively short customer lifetime (the oldest customers have ~2 years of tenure). We may not be seeing the full lifetime value if customers have longer lifecycles, and newer cohorts haven’t had time to reach their potential. Conclusions on retention and LTV trends could change with a longer observation period or more recent data.

• **Segmentation Simplicity:** We segmented by total spend only, using fixed percentile cutoffs. Real customer value segmentation might consider additional factors like profitability, frequency, or tenure. Also, the thresholds (50% and 80%) are somewhat arbitrary and may not align perfectly with business definitions (e.g. maybe the company considers the top 10% as VIPs). Nonetheless, the broad insight of value concentration holds, but exact segment definitions could be refined.

• **Data Quality:** This analysis assumes the provided data is accurate and complete. Any missing transactions, incorrect revenue attribution, or untracked marketing costs would affect the results. For example, if some marketing spend (like branding efforts) wasn’t attributed to specific channels, total ROI could be overestimated. Additionally, external factors (competition, economic conditions) that might have impacted customer behavior are not accounted for here.

• **Generalizability:** The findings and recommendations are tailored to this specific dataset and company context. While they provide valuable direction, they should be validated in practice. Before making major budget reallocations or strategy shifts, the company should pilot these changes and ensure similar results hold in the current market environment.
